01The Ideal Client

The one-paragraph answer the worksheet asks for.

Maren is a 38-year-old nurse in the Portland metro. She reads labels the way she reads charts — skeptically, and all the way through — so "clean" only means something to her when she can pronounce the ingredients and see who made them. Her weekends are trailheads and a Subaru with dog hair in the back, her vacations are National Parks, and her skin pays for all of it: wind, sun, long shifts, hard water. She buys from local boutiques and farmers markets because she'd rather know the maker than the marketing, and once something works she repurchases it for years without shopping around.

02Reality check

Where the live data agrees with the worksheet — and where it does not.

Confirmed · local works

"essance skincare portland" ranks in position 1.2 with a 32.7% click-through rate. When someone searches Essance locally, they land and they click. The PNW focus is real.

Confirmed · teens matter

A whole teen-acne content cluster already ranks. That lines up exactly with "if she has kids, they are teenagers" — she is buying for two skins, not one.

Correction · Dragon's Blood is the magnet

Nearly all non-brand search demand is Dragon's Blood, not the catalogue. It is the door people come through. The rest of the range is discovered after.

Caution · the traffic is not all her

Only 65% of clicks are US. Colombia is the #3 country. Roughly 11% comes from Spanish-speaking countries searching "sangre de drago" — real readers, but mostly not people who can buy from a small Portland brand.

Fix this line in the worksheet

The summary currently reads "mostly millennial, or women in their late 40s." Those are two different generations with different platforms, budgets and skin concerns — a 28-year-old on TikTok worried about breakouts is not a 47-year-old on Facebook worried about firmness. Pick one as primary. The rest of the worksheet points clearly at the late-30s professional, so that is what this document assumes.

04Targeting

Where the effort goes, in order.

  • Lead with the Label Reader. She has the highest repeat rate and she is the one who tells three friends. Every consumer decision gets made for her first.
  • Capture her second purchase — the teen. The content already ranks; what is missing is an obvious "for teens" path from that content into product. Cheapest available win.
  • Farm Dragon's Blood as the front door. It is what people search. Let it earn the click, then route to the full routine rather than trying to rank the whole catalogue.
  • Run wholesale as its own motion. Different message, different assets, different cadence. Do not blend it into consumer email.
  • Do not chase the international Dragon's Blood traffic. It flatters the analytics and does not convert. Judge content on US clicks, not total clicks.

05Positioning

One sentence everything else has to agree with.

For PNW women who read the label before they read the claim, Essance is the small-batch skincare house that makes plant-based, food-grade formulas by hand in Portland — because the founder still blends them herself, not a contract manufacturer.

Proof we can stand behind

Made by hand in small batches in Portland. Women-owned and minority-owned. Plant-based and cruelty-free. Food-grade, organic ingredients. Glass recycling program. Since 2011.

The hero

Dragon's Blood — a red Amazonian resin (Croton lechleri) used for centuries. Unusual enough to be a story, effective enough to be a repeat purchase.

What we do not claim

No clinical, dermatologist-tested, or medical language. No award claims we cannot cite. She is a nurse — an unsupported claim costs more trust than it buys.

The real competition

Not drugstore. It is the other small clean brand on the same boutique shelf, and the big "clean" labels with better budgets. We win on maker, not on price.

06What this means for design

Hold these

  • Real photography, always. She shops boutiques to see the actual thing. Stock or generated product imagery reads as mass-market — the exact thing she is avoiding.
  • Show texture. Open jars, swatches, what it looks like on skin. She wants to know how it feels before she pays for it.
  • Ingredients visible, not buried. Put the list where she can find it. She will look regardless; make it feel like confidence rather than fine print.
  • Natural materials, quiet palette. Cream, forest green, real paper texture. The existing brand palette is correct — the risk is drifting glossier, not staying too plain.
  • Legible over clever. She reads on a phone between shifts. Generous type, short blocks, no decorative fonts for body copy.

Avoid these

  • Clinical/lab visual language — it invites scrutiny the brand cannot support.
  • Luxury-gloss cues (foil, heavy gradients, dramatic shadow). Wrong signal for a maker brand.
  • Youth-coded design. She is not the 22-year-old TikTok skincare buyer.

07What this means for marketing

Channels that fit her

Local boutiques and farmers markets. Organic search (already working). Clean-living magazines — she reads them, which is exactly why the press pitch targets them. Instagram for texture and process, not trends.

Content that earns her

Ingredient explainers, routine order, what to use for what. Teen skincare for her kids. Founder and process content — who made this, in what room.

The objection to answer

"Why does this cost more than the drugstore?" Answer with batch size, ingredient grade and the person making it. Never answer with a discount — discounting teaches her to wait.

Practical notes

She works shifts, so send email early morning or late evening, not mid-afternoon. Travel and trail sizes suit her actual life. Seasonal skin (wind, sun, hard water) is a better hook than seasonal marketing.

08Open questions

Worth settling with Jasmine before this drives spend.

  • Millennial or Gen X? The worksheet says both. Choose the primary; the other becomes a secondary message, not an equal one.
  • Is the US Hispanic market a real target? "Sangre de drago" is culturally familiar and Dragon's Blood already ranks for it. That could be a genuine segment — or a distraction. It deserves a decision rather than drift.
  • Do we want the teen buyer explicitly? The content is already there. If yes, it needs a product path. If no, that content should route to the parent instead.
  • What is the actual repeat rate? This whole document assumes she re-buys. Worth confirming from Shopify before betting the strategy on it.
